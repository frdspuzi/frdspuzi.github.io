The Barakah Effect is the result of living life by Barakah Culture, the opposite of Hustle Culture. In Hustle Culture, your goal in life is to obtain and accumulate wealth. In Barakah Culture, you try to navigate life with Barakah.

## Introduction

### pp. 16–17

**The idea:** A business can serve a purpose beyond profit. The book argues that what makes one truly succeed is Barakah, not just good strategy.

*Does carimakanhalal have its own "riyal per chicken", a fixed commitment to Allah built into how it runs, or is the good it does just a side effect of being useful?*

carimakanhalal is, in itself, a gateway to Barakah. It shows people halal-certified places, in the hope that what they eat, and what they feed their families, is what's right. It's merely a bridge. I hope Barakah enters their lives, and mine, through the food and drink we consume.

## Ch. 1 — What is Barakah?

### pp. 40–43

**The idea:** Barakah doesn't only look like growth. It can show up as stability, as small efforts that never stop, or as things you can't see: peace, ease, and doors opening when they shouldn't.

*Where in your life do you see Barakah?*

I'd say continuity. I was about to finish my one-year graduate traineeship contract as a Validation Engineer in February 2025, without another job lined up. Alhamdulillah, Allah made it easy: I received a full-time job offer as a Software Engineer, a role I wanted to take on because of my interest in the area.

*Has that role brought you closer to Allah?*

Yes. It's mostly remote, so I get to spend more quality time at home with my family. As a trainee I was in Penang alone. Staying at home also means less time commuting, and I spent that time on projects, one of which became carimakanhalal.

*If a better-paying on-site job came up, would you stick with the role you see Barakah in, or take the money?*

Barakah can be found in different things. I see Barakah in my current job, but that doesn't mean other options can't offer Barakah too. I'd acknowledge that first, then study the other option.

*Looking back on your trainee year alone in Penang, which of the signs that Barakah is missing did you feel?*

Success that feels empty. It was a well-paying job, more than average for a fresh graduate, but I felt empty doing it. It made me realise I could have a good-paying job and still feel I'd be better off elsewhere. I was grateful for the opportunity, took it as a learning process, and came away understanding which interests I want to pursue.

*Which filled the emptiness: work that matched your interests, or being home with family?*

Honestly, both helped. In Penang I went back almost every week to see my family and friends. Day to day, I wasn't attached to what I was working on and couldn't give my best, so I chose to leave.

## Ch. 2 — How We Lost Barakah

### pp. 62–63

**The idea:** When work becomes the centre of life, it crowds out faith, family and health. The book traces this "Hustle Culture" to five pillars: materialism, individualism, capitalism, consumerism and fear of death.

*With a full-time job, freelance clients and solo side projects, are you in Hustle Culture yourself? Which of its five pillars pulls at you?*

I'd say I'm in Barakah Culture, though I want to learn more about it. My currency is pahala, good deeds. I want to accumulate that rather than wealth. I believe wealth is a by-product that eventually follows if you offer value.

*If you keep offering value and the wealth never comes, would you feel let down, or would the pahala be enough?*

Allah is as His servant thinks of Him, so we should always be optimistic about what He plans for us. "Perhaps you dislike a thing and it is good for you, and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you" ([Quran 2:216](https://quran.com/2/216)). If I keep offering value, and I can see it in the number of people using carimakanhalal, then I'd keep it up even if it costs me.
